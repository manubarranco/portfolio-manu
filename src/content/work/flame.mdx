## The assignment: a system of systems

Flame was never the brief for a design system. The brief was a system of systems: one global tier, owned centrally, and local tiers derived from it in every market, so each country could adapt the platform to its own brands and needs without fragmenting it. The job was double from day one: build the global system, and help each country build its own on top of it.

I came to it straight from Ruralvía, where we had built what I'd honestly call a proto design system. These were the early days of the discipline, and everyone doing it seriously was learning it by making it.

## My role

I joined the core design team when it was eleven people. A year in, budgets were cut, the team shrank, and its lead moved on. I took over, and from then on we were four or five: one engineer, the rest designers, running a global system for one of the world's largest banks.

The working model is the part I value most. Santander's global design lead owned every decision about the system, and she gave us her trust because we had earned it delivery by delivery. In practice: she set the objectives for the quarter, and we turned them into everything else. The plan, the months, the sprints, even what went into each sprint. She approved nearly all of it, occasionally moving a priority. That kind of autonomy is not something a client hands to a supplier team. It is earned, quarter after quarter.

## Built for adoption

A global system imposed top-down dies ignored. We knew that going in, so Flame prioritized adoption over control: a shared global foundation, and local layers where each market could adapt the system to its needs. Countries saw Flame as something usable and realistic, not as a rulebook arriving from headquarters.

> The system was an agreement, not a constraint.

Much of the work, especially in the first years, was helping countries understand the global tier and build their own on top of it. As adoption grew, the team produced fewer components and more of what makes a system consumable: documentation, contribution and distribution models, direct support to the country teams.

## One Flame: the European tier

The turning point came when several European countries set out to co-create a shared tier. I remember the forms sessions. We brought the teams together, asked each country how their forms worked, and put the European proposal next to them. The decision to converge was, in truth, mostly made before we walked in. But imposing it would have killed it. So we made our case, listened, and where a country had solved something better than the European version, we took their solution in. The tier that came out of those sessions ended up better than the global reference it derived from.

The vision we wrote down for the team that year said it plainly:

> Walk with the teams, so they help us build one Flame.

## Flame 4.0

When the European redesign was ready, the client asked us to apply it to the existing global library. I proposed something bigger: if we were going to touch everything anyway, don't patch. Break the system and rebuild it from zero.

The break made room for everything we knew the system needed. The full token architecture finally came in. I had pushed for semantic tokens long before, when the pain wasn't yet on anyone's table, and it didn't land. This time the moment had arrived. Governance moved to a hybrid model, less centralized than the original. And the adoption line became explicit: the atoms are shared, the compositions are yours. Nobody needs a thousand versions of a checkbox. A transactions list in Argentine pesos, where the amounts run far longer than any figure in euros, is a different matter. And that list is still built from the atoms everyone shares. With that argument, bringing the teams back to one shared core was an easy sell.

## What Flame proved

Flame survived five years, a European tier that outgrew its global reference, and a deliberate breaking change. It survived because it was an agreement, and agreements can be renegotiated without collapsing. The numbers are what an agreement like that leaves behind: 1,780+ components governed, 350+ designers across 10 countries, features shipping about 35% faster.
